Identity Theft: A Step-by-Step Recovery Plan

If your identity is stolen, act in this order. Freeze, report, dispute — and don't let the paperwork win.

F
FinSecure Editorial · Human-written · Updated 2026 · 15 min read
TL;DR: Identity theft recovery is a sequence, not a scramble. (1) Freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion the same day. (2) File an Identity Theft Report at IdentityTheft.gov and get your personalized recovery plan. (3) Dispute fraudulent accounts to the three bureaus with that report. (4) Secure the accounts the thief actually touched — passwords, 2FA, new cards. (5) Keep every receipt, date, and case number in one folder. Most of it takes an afternoon; the follow-ups take persistence.

About one in three American adults has experienced some form of identity theft, and the FTC's IdentityTheft.gov site sees millions of reports every year. The good news: most types of identity theft are fixable — fraudulent accounts can be removed from your credit report, and new-account fraud can be stopped cold. The bad news: the longer you wait, the more work it becomes, because fraudulent accounts age, collections start, and tax or medical fraud gets tangled in other systems.

This plan follows the FTC's recommended order, which recovery specialists repeat. Do the expensive, irreversible steps first, then the administrative ones. Panic is normal; acting in order is what fixes it.

How do you know it's identity theft?

Common signals: your bank notifies you of unusual activity, collections calls arrive for accounts you never opened, a mailer announces a new credit card you didn't request, your tax return is rejected, or your credit score suddenly drops. Sometimes there are no signals until you check your credit report — which is why the FTC recommends a free annual pull at AnnualCreditReport.com for everyone.

Step 1: Freeze your credit (same day)

Contact each national bureau online or by phone and request a credit freeze. It's free, it doesn't affect your score, and it blocks new accounts from being opened in your name. You get a PIN or password for each bureau that you'll use to unfreeze later. While you're at it, place a free initial fraud alert (90 days, renewable) on your file so lenders do identity checks before extending credit.

Red flag Anyone who contacts you on your own initiative meanwhile claiming to be the "credit bureau fraud team" and asking for your freeze PINs or SSN over the phone is a secondary scam. Scammers re-victimize. You call the bureaus; they never call you.

Step 2: Report and get your FT report

Go to IdentityTheft.gov and answer the guided questions. You'll create a personal recovery plan and — once you fill in the details — get an Identity Theft Report, which is the document creditors and bureaus accept as proof. Save your report number. If the theft involved your tax info, add the IRS steps from the same plan; if a driver's license was used, add the DMV steps.

Step 3: Secure the accounts the thief actually touched

  • Call your bank and card issuers about any fraud and get replacement cards and numbers.
  • Change passwords and enable 2FA on email, banking, and anything holding your SSN — email first, because it's the password-reset key to everything.
  • Remove the fraudulent charges from any accounts and dispute them in writing where required.
  • If any account was taken over (not just opened in your name), escalate to the bank's fraud department and document what they say.

Step 4: Dispute the fraudulent accounts

Request your free credit reports from the three bureaus, find every account that isn't yours, and dispute each one in writing, attaching your Identity Theft Report and (if you have one) a police report. Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, the bureaus must investigate. When the report confirms identity theft, they must block fraudulent accounts and remove them. Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion each have their own online dispute portal.

Why records win Keep a single folder — physical or digital — with the Identity Theft Report, every dispute confirmation and case number, emails, dates, and the names of everyone you speak to. When a collection agency or creditor resurfaces later, that folder ends the fight.

Step 5: A police report — when you need one

You don't always need a police report, but it's valuable when a creditor refuses a dispute, when you need to reissue a driver's license, or when fraud is extensive. Take your Identity Theft Report to your local department; most will write the report. The FTC's guidance explicitly supports using the Identity Theft Report in place of a police report for most creditors.

Step 6: The less-obvious aftermath

  • Tax refund theft: if your return is rejected, use the IRS Identity Protection PIN after filing your identity-theft affidavit with the IRS.
  • Medical identity theft: contact your insurer and providers so fake treatment records don't follow you.
  • Benefits fraud: if SSA, veterans', or other benefits were tapped, report to the issuing agency immediately.
  • Phone and email compromise: if the thief accessed your email, scan for password-reset emails, and revoke "remembered" devices.
Recovery red flag Any company that calls you during recovery offering to "remove identity theft from your credit file" for an upfront fee is a scam. Freezing credit and disputing fraudulent accounts are free, and the FTC's IdentityTheft.gov does the same job at no cost.

A realistic recovery timeline

It helps to know what "done" looks like. The first week is the heavy lift: freezes, the IdentityTheft.gov report, the bank and card calls, and the first wave of disputes. The first month is the follow-up loop: bureaus respond, creditors ask for the same documentation you already saved, and a few fraudulent accounts quietly drop off. After that, recovery shifts to monitoring: re-checking credit reports in month three, renewing fraud alerts, and answering the occasional letter from a collections agency that hadn't updated their records yet.

Two realistic surprises await most people. First, the bureaus and creditors will ask for the same evidence more than once — that's why the file-folder habit matters more than any legal trick. Second, the small frauds (an old address changed, a phone line opened) sometimes surface after the big new-account mess is settled, because thieves use the stolen identity in waves. Plan a three-month and a six-month checkpoint, not a one-shot cleanup.

One more honest expectation: some financial institutions take days to answer, and a few automated systems will reject a claim that a human later approves. None of that means you're doing it wrong; it means the process is built for thoroughness. Keep the copies, keep the case numbers, and treat every "no" as a request for the same paperwork routed to a different desk.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the first thing I should do if my identity is stolen?

Freeze your credit at the three bureaus, place a fraud alert, and file a report at IdentityTheft.gov as soon as you confirm theft. The very first call, though, is to the bank or card issuer where the fraud appeared.

Do I need a police report for identity theft?

Not always, but it helps. Some creditors require a police report to remove fraudulent accounts or block claims, and it creates an official record. The FTC's IdentityTheft.gov report is usually accepted in its place.

How long does it take to recover from identity theft?

Most simple cases resolve in a few weeks, but serious cases — new accounts opened in your name, tax fraud, medical identity theft — can take months or more than a year. Persistence and keeping records in one file matter most.

Can I dispute fraudulent accounts on my credit report?

Yes. Send a dispute to each credit bureau that shows the account, with your IdentityTheft.gov report and any police report. The bureaus are required to investigate and, when fraud is documented, block the account from your report.

Will a credit freeze prevent all identity theft damage?

No, but it stops the most expensive kind: new accounts opened in your name. It doesn't stop fraud on your existing accounts, tax refund fraud, or medical identity theft, which is why the full recovery plan covers all of those.

The Verdict

Recovery is real work, but it follows a boring, documented path — and that's the good news. The FTC's IdentityTheft.gov plan exists precisely so you never have to invent the process under stress. Do the three freezes in one sitting, file the report on day one, and dispute everything fraudulent in writing. Victims who keep records and follow up consistently are the ones whose recovery is measured in weeks, not years.
