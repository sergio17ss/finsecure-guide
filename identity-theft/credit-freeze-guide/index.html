Credit Freeze: The Most Powerful Free Protection

Freeze your credit at all three bureaus in about 20 minutes and stop new-account fraud cold.

F
FinSecure Editorial · Human-written · Updated 2026 · 12 min read
TL;DR: A credit freeze blocks lenders from pulling your credit file, which stops scammers from opening credit cards, loans, and phone lines in your name — the most expensive kind of identity theft. It is free, it doesn't touch your score, and it stays on until you remove it. Do it at all three bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — in one sitting, keep the PINs they give you, and lift it temporarily only when you actually apply for credit.

New-account fraud is the nightmare case of identity theft: someone opens cards and loans in your name and leaves you explaining years of damage to creditors. The single most effective defense costs nothing and takes about twenty minutes. Since September 2018, federal law requires the three national credit bureaus to offer freezes for free — placing, lifting, and removing them. The FTC recommends a freeze as routine hygiene, not just as a response to theft, and the CFPB's identity-theft guidance points to the same tool.

What a freeze actually does

A freeze restricts access to your credit report. When a lender or almost anyone tries to pull your file to check credit, the bureau refuses to share it — so the lender can't approve an application. A freeze does not remove accounts, does not affect your score, and does not stop fraud on accounts you already have. It is specifically a lock on new credit in your name.

Does a freeze hurt you? No. Your score is computed from the same history, and your existing cards keep reporting normally. The only real friction is that applying for a new card or mortgage later requires a temporary lift, which takes minutes online. That small chore is worth never having a loan you didn't sign up for.

The step-by-step, all three bureaus

  1. Set aside 20–30 minutes and make sure you can verify your identity (SSN, address history, and possibly a phone number linked to your file).
  2. Freeze at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — each has a dedicated "Security Freeze" page and a phone line. Online is fastest.
  3. Save the PIN, password, or temporary login each bureau gives you. It's your key to lift the freeze later. Do not store it in plain view on your phone.
  4. Also place a fraud alert on one bureau's file — by law they share the alert with the other two. It lasts a year and adds a verification step to any credit check in that window.
  5. Verify: request a free credit report from AnnualCreditReport.com a few weeks later.
Red flag You never pay to freeze or unfreeze. Any site that charges a fee, asks for payment before talking to a bureau, or cold-calls offering to "set up your freeze" is a scam or a rent-seeking middleman. Go to the three bureaus' official sites directly.

Notice where the real protection lives: a freeze prevents, monitoring only reports. If you can only do one thing, do the freeze — it's the one that keeps a scammer from building debt in your name in the first place.

Good times to freeze (even if nothing happened)

  • You were part of any data breach that exposed SSNs (the 2025–2026 breaches are the big ones — if in doubt, freeze anyway).
  • You lost your wallet, driver's license, or Social Security card.
  • You got a suspicious "your information was found in a breach" notice.
  • You want routine protection — freezing now is cheaper than any cleanup later.

When and how to lift it

Lifting a freeze takes minutes at any bureau, online or by phone, and you can set a precise start and end date. You can also freeze one bureau at a time to keep lenders from using the others. When you apply for credit, you only need the lift at the bureau the lender will pull. After the new card or loan is open, put the freeze right back.

The mistakes people actually make with lifts are two: lifting all three bureaus when one would do (harmless but exposed for a few hours), and forgetting to re-freeze after closing the deal. Make re-freezing part of the closing checklist — some people set a phone reminder for the same afternoon the card arrives, which is the safest interval between "nothing to lose" and "eternally frozen."

Extend protection to kids and other credit files

Children don't have credit reports until someone opens an account in their name — which is exactly why child-identity theft is so damaging and so late in being discovered. A parent or legal guardian can place a freeze on a minor's credit file at all three bureaus by mail, attaching proof of identity and guardianship; each bureau has a specific child-freeze process and response time. Adults under one roof benefit the same way: a freeze is per-file, so if the goal is protecting the whole family, every adult in the household freezes their own file in the same evening.

One more file people forget: the one held by the credit reporting agencies for fraud-audience data like ChexSystems, which banks use for checking accounts. A freeze there is less famous but is the difference between "no new credit" and "no new bank accounts" when a scammer has your number.

What lenders see during a freeze

When your file is frozen and you apply for a card, a loan, or a phone contract, the lender gets a message that your file is frozen — not a green light, not a denial. You then lift the freeze (online, in minutes) at whichever bureau the lender pulls, re-submit if needed, and once the account is open, put the freeze straight back. Your score takes no hit from the freeze itself; the small friction is the whole point of the lock.

The one exception that looks scary Some on-time services and second-look lenders use something called a "soft inquiry," which goes through even when frozen and never shows a score change. If you see a soft inquiry you didn't expect, it's worth a look — but a hard inquiry on a frozen file is the sign of an attempted application you didn't make.

Freeze doesn't stop these four things — and what does

  • Existing accounts: fraud on cards you already hold — stopped by strong passwords, 2FA, and bank alerts.
  • Tax-refund theft: blocked by an IRS Identity Protection PIN.
  • Medical identity theft: contained by asking insurers and providers to flag your records.
  • Checking-account fraud: limited by a ChexSystems freeze and bank alerts on new activity.

None of these are reasons to skip the freeze — they're the rest of the layered defense. The freeze is the foundation; the other controls fill the gaps it can't cover.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a credit freeze hurt my credit score?

No. A freeze doesn't remove accounts or change your history; it simply blocks new credit checks, so your score is untouched. Your existing accounts keep reporting normally while the freeze is on.

Does a credit freeze stop existing accounts from being used fraudulently?

No. A freeze only blocks opening new accounts in your name. Fraud on accounts you already have needs different defenses like strong passwords, 2FA, and bank monitoring alerts.

How long does a credit freeze last?

Indefinitely. It stays in effect until you lift it. A temporary lift can usually be done in minutes online or by phone, and it can be set to a specific date range.

Do I have to freeze my credit at all three bureaus?

Yes. Lenders can pull reports from any single bureau, so freezing only one or two leaves a window open. A fraud alert is different — it only lasts a year and is not a substitute for three freezes.

How much does it cost to freeze or lift a credit freeze?

Nothing. Thanks to federal requirements in place since 2018, placing, lifting, and removing a credit freeze is free at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion.

The Verdict

Credit freeze is the rare security control that is free, effective, and hard to mess up. If you've never done it, spend the afternoon now — and if you've done it once, remember it reverts to "on" only after that loan closes. Between a permanent freeze at all three bureaus and 2FA on your financial accounts, you've closed the two largest doors identity thieves use.
