The Tech Support Scam: How It Works, How to Hang Up

"Your computer has a virus" → remote access → gift cards. The full script and the one correct response.

F
FinSecure Editorial · Human-written · Updated 2026 · 10 min read
TL;DR: The tech support scam is a three-act play: a warning that your computer is "infected," a request for remote access to show you fake errors, and a demand for payment to "fix" it — always by gift card, wire, or a phony payment page. Nobody legitimate cold-calls about a virus, and no screen pop-up from a website is a real alert. The correct response to the entire script is one word: hang up — or in the browser, close the tab.

Tech support scams have been running for over a decade and still rank among the top fraud types reported to the FTC and IC3 every year, with hundreds of millions of dollars reported lost annually and a median victim loss in the hundreds. The script survives because it hits three weak spots at once: fear of losing data, respect for authority ("Windows Security Specialist"), and the instinct to be helpful.

In 2026 the script has gotten slicker — scammers use official-looking agent dashboards, legitimate remote-access tools, and AI-spoofed voices. But the anatomy is unchanged, and that's your advantage: you can learn the whole play in five minutes and never be surprised again.

Act one: the warning

It starts one of three ways:

  • A phone call: "This is Microsoft support — your computer is sending error reports."
  • A full-screen pop-up: "SYSTEM CRITICAL — your files are corrupted. Call this number immediately."
  • A browser lock in a "support" page: the page won't leave until you call.
Red flag Real security alerts are generated by your own installed antivirus and appear inside your operating system, not on a random website and never as a demand to call an unknown number.

Act two: remote access

Whoever takes the call asks you to install or open a remote-access program — TeamViewer, AnyDesk, GoToAssist, or a fake "Microsoft support" tool. Once connected, they point at Event Viewer logs, error codes, or a scripted "scan" and show you a wall of "infected files," "banking Trojans," or "child-pornography detected" to ratchet up fear.

That claim of catching something illegal against your computer is a deliberate trick: victims who fear they're implicated feel they must cooperate to prove innocence. You don't need to prove anything to a stranger.

Red flag Never install or open a remote-access tool for anyone who contacted you unsolicited. The remote session is how they browse your files, steal passwords, and record whatever you type while "paying for the fix."

Act three: the payment

Now the "repair" is quoted — commonly $199 to $499 or "lifetime protection" at three times that. Payment is accepted only by gift card, wire transfer, crypto, or a "secure checkout" page that asks for your full debit-card details. Gift cards and wires are the tell: they cannot be reversed, which is the entire reason they're demanded.

Red flag No real company accepts gift cards or wire transfers for support. "We accept Google Play / Apple / Steam gift cards for the service fee" is the tech-support scam's signature phrase. When you hear it, you're three minutes from being scammed — hang up.

The refund variant: tech support 2.0

A newer twist dials the same victim back weeks later, "from the refund department," claiming the original overcharged you and offering a refund. To "process" it, they need remote access to your banking app and ask you to read back the verification codes. This is a second withdrawal from the same account, and it's why real tech-support fraud settlements (like the FTC's cases against actual "services") always warn victims to ignore follow-up refund calls entirely.

The one correct response

  1. On a call: Do not say "yes," do not confirm your name or computer details, do not press keys "to disconnect." Just hang up. If it's a robocall, block the number.
  2. On a pop-up: Close the tab. If the page won't close, quit the browser completely or restart the machine. Then clear browser permissions.
  3. If they already got in: cut the connection — unplug from the internet or close the remote session — then remove the remote tool, run a full antivirus scan from a clean device, and change your important passwords from that clean device.
  4. If you paid: contact your bank and the gift-card issuer immediately, then report the whole event to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to IC3 at ic3.gov.
Who really helps you If you believe you have a virus, your legitimate options are known and calm: your own antivirus software, the support pages of Microsoft/Apple that you reach by typing the address yourself, and a local repair shop you choose. None of them teleport themselves into your home by phone.

The subscription and "renewal" variants

Two twists keep the script alive even after victims have heard of the basics. In the subscription variant, the scammer doesn't demand gift cards up front — they "activate your protection," email you an invoice, and drain a card later, or set up a recurring charge you'll discover in a month. In the renewal variant, the callback months later claims your "protection plan" is expiring and walks you through a renewal and a "refund" that turns out to be a second withdrawal. The defense is the same either way: no legitimate computer-maintenance company renews itself by cold-calling you, and any "refund" that requires remote access or codes is the scam again.

How to tell a real security notice from a scam

  • Source: real warnings come from your installed antivirus or operating system, in the secure area of your device — never from a website you're on, and never by phone.
  • Method: real providers push you to your own tools and your own settings. A "warning" that insists on remote access, phone contact, or a number on screen is a script.
  • Payment: real support fees are paid by card through the company's own checkout. Gift cards, wires, crypto, and "renewal" via gift codes are the scam's fingerprint.
If you want to be sure Close whatever screen or call you're facing, open your antivirus and check its real status, and if you genuinely believe there's a problem, get help from a person you chose — a local shop. The scammers' entire occupation depends on you acting before you check.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does the tech support scam work?

A caller or pop-up claims your computer is infected, gets you to install a remote-access tool, then shows "proof" of errors, and pressures you to pay for a fix or a refund — always by gift card, wire, or a fake payment page. Once they're in, they can also steal your files and passwords.

What should I do if a pop-up says my computer is infected?

Close the browser window or restart in a safe state. Legitimate antivirus alerts come from your own installed software, not from a random website and never ask you to call a number on screen or pay by gift card.

I gave a scammer remote access. What now?

Unplug from the internet, remove the remote-access program, run a full scan with your own antivirus, change your passwords from a clean device, and enable 2FA. If you paid them, report the payment to your bank and file a report with the FTC.

Will Microsoft, Apple, or my ISP call me about a virus?

No. Microsoft and Apple do not cold-call to warn about viruses, and there is no "national virus alert service" contacting homes. Anyone claiming to be tech support who calls you unsolicited is a scammer.

What should I do with a tech-support scam call?

Hang up. Do not confirm your name, do not press keys, and do not let them connect to your computer. Report the caller to the FTC and file a complaint with IC3 if you already lost money.

The Verdict

The tech support scam is the closest thing fraud has to a perfectly scripted play — which makes it the easiest to predict and refuse. No legitimate organization cold-calls about viruses, no real alert directs you to a phone number, and no legitimate tech fee is paid in gift cards. Commit the three tells to memory, practice the hang-up once so it's automatic, and this is the rare scam you can defeat with a single word.
