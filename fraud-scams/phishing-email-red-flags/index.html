Phishing 101: The 7 Red Flags in Fraudulent Emails

Train your eye with the 7 red flags that give phishing emails away — with the exact 2026 examples.

F
FinSecure Editorial · Human-written · Updated 2026 · 9 min read
TL;DR: Phishing emails get past your spam filter because they're designed to get past your attention. The seven tells that separate a real message from a fake one are: (1) the sender's real address, (2) generic or panicked greeting, (3) urgency and deadlines, (4) demands for sensitive data, (5) links that don't match the stated destination, (6) the wrong payment method, and (7) requests to bypass normal login. Spot two of those, and the right move is to close the tab and verify elsewhere.

The FTC names phishing as a top contact method for fraud, and IC3's yearly reports consistently put phishing and similar pretexting among the top incident types reported to the FBI. The emails keep working because they don't need to fool a security expert — they need to fool a tired person checking email on a phone between meetings.

The good news is that the mechanics have barely changed. A phishing email tries to create a situation you've never seen before (your account is "at risk"!) and a solution you've never used before (log in through this link / confirm a code / approve this invoice). Real institutions don't do that, and once you know the seven tells, fake messages become easy to spot.

Red flag #1: The sender's real address

The display name can be anything: "Chase Fraud Department" is just text. The true address behind it is the whole ballgame. A scammer will use chasing-support@mail.chase-secure.net, chaseonline.com.net, or a domain that looks like the real one with one letter swapped. Hover over — or on mobile, tap and hold — the sender name to reveal the actual domain.

How to check it Open the email, click the sender's name, and read the full address. The characters after the @ are the only thing that matters. If they don't exactly match the company's real domain, it's phishing.

Red flag #2: A generic, panicked greeting

"Dear Customer," "Dear Valued Member," or a subject line like "UNUSUAL ACTIVITY — ACT NOW" for services that know your name and never write like that. Real banks address you by name and don't use ALL CAPS panic in subjects.

Red flag #3: Manufactured urgency

"Your account will be closed within 24 hours," "Your email storage is full," "Final notice — response required today." The point of the deadline is to prevent you from calling the real company or asking a second person. There is no such thing as a legitimate 15-minute financial decision.

Why it works Scammers add deadlines because verification is the enemy. If the deadline says "today" and your bank's real phone line has a hold time, the email wins. The correct reply to any deadline in a suspicious email is "then I'll take the deadline by calling the official number."

Red flag #4: A request for information no real company needs

Your password, your full SSN, your one-time login code, your card PIN, or your full account number — no legitimate institution asks for these by email, ever. If an email asks you to "confirm" any of them, it's the scam's whole purpose.

Red flag #5: Links that don't match the stated destination

The visible text says "Secure login to your account," but the link behind it goes somewhere else entirely. Hover before you click: the status bar shows the destination URL. If it isn't the exact official domain, it's phishing. You can always type the official address yourself instead of trusting the link.

Red flag #6: The wrong payment method

The invoice or refund asks for payment "by gift card," "by wire," or "reimbursement" through a peer-to-peer app. Real companies bill through their own checks and systems; they never demand gift cards or Zelle. If the email steers you to a payment app, it's a fraud template.

Red flag #7: Instructions to bypass normal logins

"To unlock, reply with the code we just texted," "use the temporary password below," "log in via this link instead of the app." Legitimate systems push you toward their standard methods. Requests to do something different are requests to hand over your keys.

What a real email looks like Real financial emails address you by name, never ask for secrets, link to domains you can verify, and never force an impossible deadline. When you're unsure, the answer is the same every time: don't click — go to the app or the number on your card.

You clicked. Now what?

  1. Don't type anything into the page if you haven't already.
  2. If you entered a password, change it immediately from a clean device and enable 2FA.
  3. If you downloaded an attachment, run a malware scan — never open unexpected "invoices" or ".docm" files.
  4. Report the email to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and forward it to your email provider's abuse desk.
  5. If you gave up a one-time code, contact the affected service and your bank fast.

Beyond email: vishing and smishing

Phishing's cousins are vishing (voice phishing by phone call) and smishing (phishing by text). The same tells apply, translated: a caller who claims to be from your bank but calls you first; a text with a link that "verifies" a delivery or a "fraud attempt." Smishing is especially effective because texts feel more personal and urgent, and tapping a link on a phone hides the real domain behind the address bar.

Phone-specific red flags Anyone who calls you claiming to be a company and asks for a code, a password, or remote access is running a script. Hang up and call the official number on their site or your card — the call you place yourself is the only one you can trust.

Real 2026 examples to practice on

  • "Your Zelle payment of $450 is pending." The text includes a link to "cancel" — a lookalike login page collects your credentials. Real Zelle confirmations live in the app, and a texted link is never how confirmations arrive.
  • "Invoice #2026-88412 from your supplier — details attached." The attachment is a macro file. Suppliers don't send surprise invoices to your personal inbox.
  • "Your tax refund is on hold — restore by signing in." The IRS never emails about refund holds and never asks for your SSN in an email link.
  • "Your package could not be delivered." The tracking link points to a payment-app page asking for a "redelivery fee." Legitimate carriers charge nothing to re-deliver.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common red flag in a phishing email?

The sender's actual email address. Display names can look perfect, but the real domain behind the address is usually misspelled or slightly off — like support@yourbank-secure.net instead of yourbank.com.

What should I do if I clicked a phishing link?

Do not type any credentials on the page. Close the tab, change the passwords of the accounts you almost used, run a scan if you downloaded anything, and report the email to the FTC. If you did enter a password, treat the account as compromised immediately.

How can I verify that an email is really from my bank?

Never click the link. Log in to your bank app directly or call the number on the back of your card. Real financial institutions never ask you to "confirm" your password, SSN, or one-time code by email.

Do phishing emails always have typos and bad grammar?

Not anymore. Many 2026 campaigns are polished and personalized. Typos are a useful negative signal, but the real tests are the sender domain, the urgency, the method they want you to use, and the sensitive data they request.

What is the safest action when an email asks me to act urgently?

Assume it's a scam until verified. Real emergencies are handled by phone calls and official apps, not email links. Take your time, verify through a separate channel, and you have already dodged the attack.

The Verdict

Phishing doesn't scale because the emails are sophisticated — it scales because one message out of ten thousand lands when someone is distracted. The seven red flags above give you a fast checklist that takes fifteen seconds. Practiced once a week or so, that checklist becomes the reflex that turns a would-be takeover into an afternoon of forwarding a suspicious email to your provider and the FTC.
